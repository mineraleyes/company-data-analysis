# ASX — The numbers

*726 mining companies, A$1,079B, as at 26 August 2026. Population and market cap from the ASX export; commodity, geography and stage derived from Yahoo Finance.*

## Headline

**One company is a third of the sector. The top three are more than half.**

| | ASX mining |
|---|---|
| Companies | 726 |
| Total market cap | A$1,079.4B |
| Median market cap | A$32.4M |

## Concentration

**The ASX is markedly more concentrated than either Canadian board.** The TSX's mining top ten are 54.8% of that board; here the top ten are **74.2%**, and BHP, Newmont and Fortescue are 56% between them.

That is a different market structure, not a bigger version of the same one. Canada's weight sits across a few dozen mid-to-large producers; Australia's sits in three global majors above a very long tail.

## Lifecycle stage

**115 companies sell metal. They are 16% of the miners and 93% of the value.**

| | Companies | Median market cap | Share of value | Est. turnover |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Producer | 115 | A$646.1M | 92.9% | 24.8% |
| Explorer | 576 | A$22.8M | 6.5% | 13.2% |
| Developer | 15 | A$97.2M | 0.3% | 15.4% |
| Royalty / streamer | 3 | A$1,138.3M | 0.2% | 23.4% |
| Unknown | 17 | A$24.0M | 0.1% | 9.6% |

**The concentration of value in producers is more extreme than on the TSX** — 93% against 72% — even though producers are a similar slice of the company count (16% here, 12% there). Australia's producers are simply much larger relative to their juniors.

**Explorer trading is genuinely thinner, not just smaller.** Estimated turnover runs 13.2% for explorers against 24.8% for producers: roughly half the churn per dollar of market value. The TSX shows the same direction at a higher level (29.3% against 43.8%), but the two turnover figures are built differently and should not be read against each other — see the caveats.

**Developer is a real bucket here, and it was not on the TSX.** Fifteen companies have construction in progress on the balance sheet: Core Lithium, Alpha HPA, Galan, Talga, Hastings and ten others. On the TSX side this could not be built at all, because "feasibility" appears once across 1,079 business summaries. Financial statements answered a question prose could not.

## Size distribution

**44% of the sector is below A$25M, and those 319 companies are worth A$3.7B between them — 0.35% of the total.**

{{chart:asx_size}}

The shape mirrors the TSXV: very many very small companies, essentially none of the value. The difference is that Australia keeps its majors and its shells on one board, so this single distribution spans what takes two exchanges in Canada — 41 companies above A$2B hold A$986B, and 46 below A$5M hold A$0.2B.

## Commodity

**Gold is named by 70% of ASX miners. Copper by 52%. Lithium by 31%.**

{{chart:asx_commodity}}

These sixteen mirror the TSX chart exactly, and the contrast is the point:

| | ASX | TSX / TSXV |
|---|---|---|
| Gold | 70% | 61% |
| Copper | 52% | 33% |
| Lithium | 31% | 10% |
| Rare earths | 20% | 3% |

**Lithium and rare earths are where the two markets diverge most.** Lithium is named by nearly a third of ASX miners against a tenth on the TSXV; rare earths by a fifth against 3%. Australia's speculative money sits in battery and critical minerals in a way Canada's does not.

Companies name more than one commodity, so the bars sum to well beyond 726 — 554 name two or more. Only 11 name none.

### Commodities with no TSX counterpart

{{chart:asx_commodity_extra}}

Cobalt is named by 105 companies, niobium and tantalum by 39, graphite by 34. The TMX flag set has no column for any of these, so they cannot be compared like for like — which is itself a finding about the two exports.

## Where the assets are

**97% name Australian ground. 46% also name something offshore.**

{{chart:asx_assets}}

Africa (13%) edges out Canada (12%) and the USA (11%) as the most-named offshore destination — West African gold, mostly. That is a genuine structural difference from the TSX, where the offshore tail runs to Latin America first.

**These counts understate, by construction.** They come from business summaries, which typically name the flagship project and stop. The TSX side has a property register listing every jurisdiction a company holds ground in; this does not. Treat the offshore figures as a floor.

Rare earths at 8% and platinum at 1% are the speculative extremes — 145 companies naming rare earths and eleven of them selling anything.

## Listings by year

**2021 was extraordinary: 103 of the surviving miners listed that year, more than the previous eight combined.**

The battery-metals float wave. 2022 added 58 more, then it stopped: 24 in 2023 and 13 in 2024.

96 surviving companies listed before 2000 and are not shown on the chart; the oldest dates to 1885.

### Was 2021 a bad vintage?

**Yes, but you could not have told for eighteen months.**

| Held for | 2021 cohort | Every other year |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | −30% (74% down) | −26% (68% down) |
| 2 years | −50% (80% down) | −48% (75% down) |
| 3 years | **−75% (92% down)** | −52% (75% down) |

At one year the 2021 floats look like any other cohort. At three years they are 23 points worse and **92 of every 100 are below their listing price** — the highest failure rate of any cohort in the file. The lithium and rare-earth floats of 2021 raised money at the top of the battery-metals cycle and the price turned before most of them had drilled out a resource.

## What happens after listing

**The median ASX miner is down 27% a year after listing, 68% after five, and 93% after fifteen.**

The full curve is on the Charts tab, where it can be split by stage. The headline is that it is *the same curve as Canada's*:

| Held for | ASX | TSX / TSXV |
|---|---|---|
| 1 month | −4.6% | −4.8% |
| 6 months | −15.0% | −17.7% |
| 1 year | −26.5% | −26.7% |
| 2 years | −47.9% | −47.9% |
| 5 years | −68.1% | −66.5% |
| 10 years | −88.7% | −82.3% |
| 15 years | −93.1% | −92.6% |

**Two exchanges, different commodity mixes, different regulators, near-identical decay.** This is the one cross-market comparison in the report that is honestly like for like — same script, same milestones, same base, and a return is a ratio, so the currencies never meet. Australia is slightly worse in the tail and consistently a few points worse on the share of companies underwater, but the shape is not distinguishable.

### Producers and explorers do not just differ — they invert

| Held for | Producer | Explorer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | −12% | −27% |
| 5 years | −60% | −69% |
| 10 years | −57% | −91% |
| 15 years | **−37%** | **−95%** |

The explorer line falls monotonically to near-total loss. The producer line bottoms out around year five and then **recovers**.

**Read this carefully, because it is not "buy producers".** Stage is measured today, not at listing. A company that floated as an explorer and became a producer is counted as a producer for its entire price history, so the producer line is a description of the companies that succeeded, not evidence that buying producers works. What it does establish is that the upside in this sector is real but concentrated: the middle producer at fifteen years is down 37%, while the 75th percentile is up 274%.

### The rollback tells the story

**187 of 611 priced companies — 31% — have consolidated their shares since listing.** The median consolidation is 25 old shares into 1.

| | n | median at 5 years |
|---|---|---|
| Consolidated | 183 | −80% |
| Not | 251 | −55% |

A share consolidation is a symptom, not a cause: a company rolls back when the price has fallen so far that it is at risk of the exchange's minimum. It is the single cheapest flag in the file for a company that has already gone badly wrong.

### Commodity barely matters

At three years the spread across commodities runs from iron at −44% to uranium at −69%, and **lithium (−56%) is no worse than gold (−58%)** despite the lithium price collapse. What a company said it was looking for explains very little about what happened to its share price. Listing year and reaching production explain far more.

## Caveats

- **The population is derived, not given.** 726 of 775 GICS Materials companies, using Yahoo's industry where it names a mining industry and the business summary otherwise. 49 were excluded — Amcor, James Hardie, BlueScope, Orica, Dyno Nobel, Sims and 43 smaller names, A$100B in total.
- **Coal and uranium are partly present.** Excluding the Energy sector removed Yancoal and Whitehaven but left 13 coal and uranium miners that GICS files under Materials. The TMX population carries coal and uranium in full, so the two are not defined the same way.
- **Commodity and geography come from prose.** They record what a company says it explores for, which is not what it holds, and summaries go stale. The TSX equivalents come from a TMX register.
- **Estimated turnover is not TSX turnover.** The ASX export has no trading data, so it is average daily volume × price × 126 trading days ÷ market cap. TMX reports dollars actually traded. Same quantity, different construction — never put them on one axis.
- **Australian dollars, never converted.** An A$ size band is not the C$ band with the same label.
- **21 companies have no market cap** and 17 no financial statements at all.
- **Survivorship, and it bites hardest on the returns.** Only companies listed on 26 August 2026 exist in the source. Every listing-year count is of survivors, and every return is a return on a company that is still here — the ones that failed outright are absent, so the real picture is worse than the one above at every horizon.
- **Returns cover 611 of 726 miners.** 99 have no Yahoo history reaching back to their listing, and 73 of those listed before 2000 — Yahoo simply has no 1980s and 1990s ASX data. A further 16 are excluded as suspected backdoor listings.
- **Stage on the returns chart is measured today, not at listing.** See the producer/explorer note above; this is the largest interpretive trap in the section.
- **One company lost twenty-one years to a data fault.** AIC Mines' Yahoo adjusted-close series is negative from 1993 to 2014, because A$5.49 of dividends are back-adjusted through a 12:1 and then a 20:1 consolidation. Non-positive quotes are dropped rather than repaired, so seven companies lose part of their history and AIC Mines drops out of the returns entirely.
